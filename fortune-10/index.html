The ten largest U.S. public companies by revenue share the same tier of the Fortune list — and almost nothing else. Scale explains who is at the top. Margin, capital efficiency, and cash generation explain who is actually winning.

01 — Executive Summary

Size and strength are not the same thing

Together these ten companies booked $4,157B of revenue and $422B of net income in their most recent fiscal year. But the revenue ranking and the profit ranking are nearly inverted at the extremes: the #1 and #2 companies by revenue (Walmart, Amazon) earn thinner margins than three companies ranked below them.

The five patterns that matter

  1. Revenue rank ≠ profit rank. Walmart leads revenue at $681B but nets 3.0%. Alphabet, ranked 7th by revenue, earns the most profit of any company here ($100.1B).
  2. Profit is far more concentrated than revenue. The top three revenue firms hold 41% of revenue; the top three profit firms hold 67% of all net income.
  3. Business model sets the margin ceiling. Technology platforms clear 24–29% net margins; health-care distributors (McKesson, Cencora, CVS) operate on 0.5–1.2%.
  4. Capital efficiency splits the field. Apple returns 26% on assets; Berkshire, sitting on $1,154B of assets, returns 7.8%.
  5. Distributors run on negative or razor-thin equity. McKesson carries negative book equity; Cencora's is near zero — they fund enormous throughput with supplier float, not capital.

03 — Revenue Scale

Who is largest

The ten companies cluster remarkably tightly: from Walmart at $681B down to Cencora at $294B, the entire list fits inside a 2.3× revenue band. Five distinct business models are represented, and health-care & distribution alone accounts for four of the ten names.

Concentration is modest: the top three (Walmart, Amazon, UnitedHealth) generate 41% of combined revenue. Revenue, in other words, is spread fairly evenly — which makes the profit picture all the more lopsided.

04 — Profitability

Who converts revenue into earnings

Net income tells a completely different story from revenue. Alphabet and Apple — 7th and 4th by sales — are 1st and 3rd by profit. Berkshire Hathaway, 6th by revenue, is 2nd by profit. Meanwhile CVS, McKesson, and Cencora move hundreds of billions in revenue while keeping less than 1.5 cents of every dollar.

A small cluster of high-margin firms dominates earnings: Apple, Alphabet, and Berkshire together produce 67% of the group's net income while generating only 27% of its revenue.
05 — The Rank Mismatch

Revenue, profit, and market value rank differently

Reading left to right, each line tracks one company's position across three ladders. Lines that plunge or climb steeply are companies the market values very differently from where Fortune ranks them. Apple and Alphabet rise; Walmart, CVS, and the distributors fall away on profit and valuation.

06 — Growth Momentum

Who has momentum

Growth does not follow size. The distributors and Alphabet posted double-digit revenue growth; the energy and conglomerate names were roughly flat. Amazon stands out as the only mega-scale company still compounding revenue above 10%.

The more revealing split is growth in profit: Amazon nearly doubled net income year over year (+95%) and Alphabet grew it +36%, while UnitedHealth and CVS saw profit fall sharply on medical-cost pressure and one-time charges.

07 — Market Valuation

How the market prices them

The stock market rewards future economics, not current sales. Apple and Alphabet carry trillion-dollar-plus valuations on revenue that ranks mid-pack here. The distributors and CVS — huge by revenue — command the smallest market values, reflecting their thin margins and limited pricing power.

Investors pay roughly 8.2× sales for Apple and 6.0× for Alphabet, versus under 0.2× for McKesson and Cencora. The market is pricing margin durability and growth optionality, not revenue scale.
08 — Productivity

Revenue per employee

Workforce scale and workforce productivity are opposite axes. Walmart and Amazon employ over 1.5 million people each; Cencora and McKesson move comparable revenue with ~50,000. Per head, the asset-light distributors and energy look extraordinarily productive on revenue — but that revenue is almost all pass-through cost of goods.

Profit per employee corrects the illusion: Apple generates roughly $0.57M of net income per employee and Alphabet $0.55M, while Walmart earns under $10K and the distributors a few thousand dollars per head.

09 — Capital Efficiency

Asset intensity vs. margin

This is the clearest map of the group. The vertical axis is net margin; the horizontal is asset turnover (how much revenue each dollar of assets produces). Bubble size is revenue. The four quadrants sort the field into recognizable archetypes.

Apple's 26% return on assets is more than triple Walmart's and more than ten times CVS's. Berkshire, holding $1,154B in assets — the largest base here by far — converts them at just 7.8%, the trade-off of a diversified insurance-and-investment model.

10 — Cash Generation

Free cash flow separates durable from accounting performers

Cash is harder to manufacture than reported profit. Apple converts net income to operating cash almost dollar-for-dollar and throws off the most free cash flow of any company here. Amazon and Alphabet generate enormous operating cash but plow much of it back into capex — data centers, logistics, and AI infrastructure.

The asset-light distributors spend almost nothing on capex (under 0.3% of revenue) but generate little cash because margins are so thin. The capital-intensive names — Alphabet, Amazon, Exxon — spend the most, a deliberate bet on future capacity rather than a weakness.

11 — Balance-Sheet Risk

Leverage and liquidity

Financial risk cannot be read from revenue. The leverage profiles range from Alphabet's near-debt-free balance sheet to the distributors' thin equity bases. A current ratio below 1.0 — common here — is normal for firms that run on supplier credit, but it signals reliance on continuous cash flow.

Apple, Walmart, and the distributors all run current ratios below 1.0 — they are funded by payables and turn inventory faster than bills come due. Alphabet (1.84×) and Exxon (1.31×) carry the most liquidity cushion.

13 — Conclusion

Scale gets you on the list; quality decides the rest

The 2025 Fortune 10 proves that America's largest companies are not financially similar just because they are close in revenue. Across the lenses examined here, leadership keeps changing hands:

  • Revenue: Walmart ($681B).
  • Profit: Alphabet ($100.1B).
  • Margin & capital efficiency: Apple (24.0% margin, 26% ROA).
  • Free cash flow: Apple ($109B).
  • Growth: Amazon among mega-caps; the distributors on a smaller base.
  • Market value: Apple and Alphabet, on economics rather than scale.

Revenue scale explains who appears at the top of the Fortune ranking. Margin quality, capital efficiency, cash generation, and growth expectations explain which of these companies actually create the most financial value — and on every one of those measures, the revenue leaders are not the leaders.

For deeper analysis: segment-level margins (AWS vs. Amazon retail; UnitedHealth's Optum vs. insurance), multi-year trend lines, and a true market-cap pull from a market-data feed to replace the estimates used in the valuation and productivity sections.
