01 Background

How California built the largest ledger in the union

A budget bigger than most countries, balanced on a tax base narrower than almost any other state.

Every January, the governor of California unveils a document that would be a national budget almost anywhere else on earth. The state's General Fund — the pot of discretionary money lawmakers actually fight over — now moves roughly $230 billion a year. Counted in full, with federal funds and special accounts, the enterprise clears half a trillion dollars.

That scale is the product of a singular economy. If California were a country, it would rank among the five largest on the planet, ahead of the United Kingdom. The tech fortunes of Silicon Valley, the entertainment machine of Los Angeles, the farms of the Central Valley and the ports of the coast all feed one treasury. But the way that treasury is filled — and the rules that govern how it is spent — were written in a handful of ballot-box revolts that still define every budget today.

The first was Proposition 13 in 1978, the taxpayer rebellion that capped property taxes and required two-thirds supermajorities to raise many others. Overnight, the steadiest revenue source available to most governments — property tax — was frozen for California. The state leaned instead on the one lever still within easy reach: the personal income tax.

The second revolt cut the other way. Proposition 98, passed in 1988, locked in a constitutional guarantee that roughly 40 cents of every General Fund dollar flow to K–12 schools and community colleges. A later measure, Proposition 2 in 2014, forced the state to sweep a share of revenue into a constitutional rainy-day fund. Each was a reasonable idea on its own. Stacked together, they produced a budget that is enormous, heavily pre-committed, and acutely sensitive to the fortunes of a few thousand very wealthy taxpayers.

California doesn't have a spending problem or a revenue problem so much as a volatility problem. The money arrives in a flood or a drought, and rarely in between. — A recurring theme across four decades of Legislative Analyst reports

To understand why California can announce a $97 billion surplus one spring and a $45 billion shortfall the next, you have to follow the money — where it comes from, where it is promised, and how violently it swings. That is the story of the next four chapters.

02 The Problem

A budget built on the stock market

When revenue rides on the capital gains of the wealthiest Californians, the budget rises and falls with Wall Street.

Step 01 — The climb

Revenue has more than tripled since 2000.

The General Fund grew from about $71 billion in 2000 to roughly $230 billion today. On a chart, the long arc looks like a triumph — a state economy compounding for a generation.

Step 02 — The whiplash

But the line is jagged, not smooth.

Look closer and the climb is broken by violent drops and spikes: a collapse after the 2008 financial crisis, a pandemic-era surge to a record in 2021–22, then a sharp reversal. Revenue can swing tens of billions in a single year.

Step 03 — The driver

Almost all the volatility lives in one source.

Sales tax (the steady band below) barely moves. It's the personal income tax — and within it, the capital gains of high earners — that lurches up and down, dragging the whole budget with it.

Step 04 — The exposure

Capital gains are the wild card.

Taxable capital gains swung from about $60B in 2009 to nearly $240B at the 2021 peak. The state taxes those gains as ordinary income — so a good year on Wall Street becomes a surplus, and a bad one becomes a deficit.

03 The Money

Where it comes from, where it goes

A treasury fed by a handful of taxes, then spent down two enormous commitments — schools and health care.

Follow a single budget dollar and the structure becomes clear. It arrives overwhelmingly through one door and leaves through two. Hover the charts below to read the figures.

This is why California's choices feel so constrained even when the budget is vast. Roughly 40% is pre-promised to schools by Proposition 98. A third more funds Medi-Cal and the rest of health and human services, much of it tied to federal match requirements that are painful to cut. What remains — higher education, courts, parks, the bureaucracy, the discretionary programs that make headlines — is a sliver fighting over the part of the budget that actually flexes.

In a good year, the surplus is real but temporary. In a bad year, the cuts fall on the smallest, least-protected slice of the budget.

That structural mismatch — volatile income on one side, rigid commitments on the other — is exactly what the rainy-day fund was built to absorb.

04 The Research

What four decades of analysis keeps finding

The diagnosis is remarkably stable across administrations. The disagreement is only about the cure.

Strip away the politics and the independent analysis — from the Legislative Analyst's Office, academic public-finance researchers, and the Department of Finance's own forecasts — converges on a short list of structural findings.

Finding 01

Volatility is structural, not accidental

Tying revenue to top-bracket income — especially capital gains — guarantees boom-and-bust cycles regardless of who is in office. The swing is a feature of the tax base, not a failure of management.

Finding 02

Surpluses are mostly temporary

Peak-year windfalls are dominated by one-time capital gains that don't recur. Spending them on permanent programs converts a one-year surplus into a multi-year structural deficit.

Finding 03

Reserves help, but can't fully absorb the swing

The rainy-day fund meaningfully softened recent downturns, but a reserve sized in the low tens of billions cannot offset a revenue swing of comparable size. It buys time, not immunity.

Finding 04

Rigidity compounds the pain

Because so much spending is formula-locked, downturns concentrate cuts on a narrow discretionary slice — making the cuts deeper and more disruptive than the overall numbers suggest.

If the diagnosis is settled, the prescriptions are not. Researchers and budget officials have circled the same family of structural reforms for years — each with a real trade-off between stability, fairness, and political feasibility.

Reform A

Broaden the tax base

Lean less on volatile top-bracket income by widening sales tax to services or rebalancing toward steadier sources. Reduces the swing — but raises hard questions about who pays.

Stability impact: HighPolitical feasibility: Low
Reform B

Wall off capital-gains windfalls

Automatically divert above-trend capital-gains revenue straight into reserves and one-time uses, so peak-year surges never fund permanent commitments.

Stability impact: Medium-HighPolitical feasibility: Medium
Reform C

Deepen and unlock the reserve

Raise the rainy-day cap and loosen the rules on when it can be spent, so the buffer is large enough — and flexible enough — to ride out a full downturn.

Stability impact: MediumPolitical feasibility: Medium-High
Reform D

Budget to the trend, not the peak

Set ongoing spending against a conservative long-run revenue estimate, treating everything above it as one-time. Boring, durable, and the reform researchers most consistently endorse.

Stability impact: HighPolitical feasibility: Medium

The Forty-Year Whiplash

05 What Comes Next

The richest state keeps writing the same lesson

California's budget is not broken so much as it is honest — a perfect mirror of an economy that produces staggering wealth in unpredictable bursts. The same structure that delivers a record surplus in a boom guarantees a painful gap in the bust that follows.

Four decades of analysis point to the same uncomfortable conclusion: the volatility cannot be forecast away, only absorbed — through deeper reserves, a broader base, and the discipline to treat windfalls as the temporary gifts they are. None of that is technically hard. All of it is politically difficult, which is why the lesson keeps getting re-learned, surplus after deficit after surplus.

The next downturn is not a question of if. The only open question is whether the state will have saved enough, in the good years, to meet it.
